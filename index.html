Executive summary

This guide is Project Output4 (as named in the Grant Agreement) of the TalentTECH project, co-funded by the European Union under the Erasmus+ Sport programme (Grant Agreement No. 101235717). It is a step-by-step manual for football academies, coaches and scouts on how to implement and use three kinds of sports technology: AI tools for scouting and player evaluation, wearable sensors for monitoring, and video analysis systems.

The guide follows the Grant Agreement. For each technology it gives technical setup instructions, best practices for data interpretation, recommendations for integrating the tool into training routines, and troubleshooting tips. It is written for small and medium academies with different levels of digital readiness. The players they work with are young, and many are minors.

The guide rests on three points:

  • Start with a problem. Chapter 2 helps you decide what you need before you buy anything.
  • Numbers describe; people decide. AI scores, load numbers and video tags are inputs. A qualified person takes every decision about a young player.
  • Same routine every time. Most data problems come from a missed step. The numbered procedures in chapters 3 to 5 and the checklists in Annex A keep the routine stable.

The guide is published under a CC BY-SA 4.0 licence as a printable PDF and a web page (https://talenttech-project.github.io/d2-4/). Four short video walkthroughs accompany the guide (section 1.4).

Introduction

1 Introduction

1.1 Purpose and scope of the guide

This guide shows you how to put three technologies to work in a football academy for player evaluation and performance enhancement:

  • AI tools that support scouting and player evaluation (chapter 3);
  • wearable sensors for monitoring load, health and injury prevention (chapter 4);
  • video analysis systems for tactical and technical evaluation (chapter 5).

It is a manual. The theory behind each technology is in the TalentTECH curriculum (D2.1) and the e-learning modules (D2.2). Real examples from clubs are in the case studies (D2.3). This guide tells you what to do, in what order, and how to check that it worked.

1.2 Target audience

The guide is written for football academies, coaches and scouts. It also serves performance staff (fitness coaches, sports scientists), academy managers and coordinators, and analysts. The quick-start checklists at the end of chapters 3 to 5 are split by role.

We assume a small or medium academy with limited budget and staff time. Where a step costs money, the guide gives a small-academy option. Where a step touches player data, the guide points to the data protection rules in section 2.5 and Annex C. Technology should also serve female players and players with disabilities: fit, feedback and consent routines should be checked for every group you work with.

Preparation

2 Before you start: preparing the academy

The D2.3 case studies show that academies which started with a clear problem, a named data owner and a realistic budget kept using their tools; others did not. The lesson: technology projects often fail before the first device is switched on. This chapter covers the five decisions to take first.

2.1 Start with a specific problem, not with the technology

Write down the one problem you want to solve this season. Examples:

  • "Our U15 scouts rate the same player very differently." (chapter 3)
  • "We do not know how hard last week was for each player." (chapter 4)
  • "Players do not understand our feedback on positioning." (chapter 5)

Then write how you will know it is solved. Use something you can count: for example, two scouts agreeing more often, or every player having a load record for every session. D2.3 (section 14.3) draws the same lesson from the Lithuanian cases: begin with a problem and an implementation plan, not with a purchase.

Check

Your problem statement fits in two sentences, and a colleague can repeat it back to you.

2.3 Budget, staff time and choosing sustainable tools

Staff time is the cost academies forget to budget; licence fees are only the visible part. For each tool, estimate the hours per week for setup, data download, checking and reporting, and name who will do them. D2.3's practitioner cases found that budget and the coach's own preferences decide whether a tool is adopted, and that planning for staff time matters as much as paying for licences.

Rules for a small academy:

  • Choose tools you can sustain for three seasons. Advanced features you cannot keep paying for are worth less than a tool you still use in season three.
  • Remove repetitive paperwork first. D2.3 (Table 14, Cases 8 and 9) draws the lesson "admin first, performance tech second": remove repetitive paperwork before adding sensors or AI. If staff are drowning in admin, a performance tool will not be used.
  • Pilot before you commit. One age group, one season, then decide (section 7.4).
Small-academy option

Each technology chapter opens with a no-cost or low-cost route: session-RPE instead of sensors, a phone camera instead of an automated camera, and a structured scouting template instead of an AI tool. Each builds a routine you will need later anyway.

2.5 Data protection and safeguarding principles for youth players

Every tool in this guide collects personal data about young players. The rules below are principles. Annex C turns them into a checklist. This section is not legal advice: confirm each point with your data protection officer (DPO) or legal adviser, and with your national data protection authority's guidance.

  1. Follow the GDPR principles. Data must be processed lawfully, fairly and transparently, for clear purposes, limited to what is needed, kept accurate, kept no longer than necessary, and kept secure. The academy must be able to show it does this (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Art. 5).
  2. Decide the legal basis first. Consent is one of six legal bases. Decide and document the basis for each use of data before you start (GDPR, Art. 6). If a player or parent feels that refusing a sensor or a camera would hurt their place in the squad, consent may not be "freely given". Ask your DPO or data protection contact (section 2.4) which basis fits, and make withdrawal as easy as giving consent (EDPB, 2020a).
  3. Involve parents for minors. The GDPR sets a "digital age of consent" of 13 to 16, depending on the country. It applies only to online services offered directly to children, for example a player app the child signs up to. All other processing needs its own legal basis and, for minors, normally involves parents. Check the age in your country with your national data protection authority. Verify parental consent in a proportionate way. When a player reaches the national age, tell them they can confirm or withdraw the consent their parents gave (EDPB, 2020a; European Commission, n.d.).
  4. Treat sensor data as health data. The GDPR's definition of health data is broad. Physiological measurements can count as health data whatever device produced them (GDPR, Art. 4(15), Art. 9; Article 29 Working Party, 2015). Heart-rate and other physiological data collected over a season are likely to be health data. Identify an Article 9 exception (for example explicit consent) with your DPO before collecting it.
  5. Do a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) when in doubt. Art. 35 GDPR requires a DPIA for high-risk processing, and national authorities publish lists. In a survey of over 100 sports clubs by Ireland's data protection authority, 9% had done a DPIA and 56% had no retention schedule (DPC, 2024).
  6. A person takes every decision. Under Art. 22 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), people have a right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing. Recital 71 adds that such decisions should not concern a child. So an AI tool must not decide on its own whether a young player is selected or released: a qualified person reviews the evidence and takes the decision. Chapter 3 applies this rule to scouting tools.
  7. Explain it to players in simple words. What is collected, why, who sees it, and how long it is kept (GDPR, Art. 12–13).
  8. Video: analysis is not publication. Ordinary match video is personal data but not automatically biometric data; if a tool uses face recognition, stricter rules apply (EDPB, 2020b). Before posting any clip of a youth player online, have a clear legal basis (usually parental consent), say where it will appear and for how long, and allow withdrawal (DPC, n.d.).
  9. Secure the data. Use player codes instead of names where you can, protect accounts with strong passwords, encrypt laptops and phones, keep backups, and check security regularly (GDPR, Art. 32). Where a supplier stores the data, the contract should say who is controller, who is processor and where the data is kept.
  10. Know which law applies. Partners in Türkiye follow Law No. 6698 on the Protection of Personal Data (KVKK), which also treats health data as a special category (KVKK, 2016). In this guide, KVKK refers to the law; the authority of the same name publishes guidance on it.
Watch out: AI tools and the EU AI Act

An academy that uses an AI scouting tool is a "deployer" under Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the AI Act). Article 4 asks deployers to take measures to support AI literacy among the staff who use the tool. Article 4 has applied since 2 February 2025; its wording was amended in July 2026 by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744. The AI Act does not list football scouting as a high-risk use. Some functions, such as emotion recognition or biometric identification, are regulated, so check what a tool does before you buy it. Do not describe scouting AI as "high-risk" unless legal advice confirms it.

3.1 Overview: what AI tools can and cannot do

An AI scouting tool learns patterns from past data, for example which players an academy selected in earlier seasons, and applies them to new players. Its output is usually a score, a ranking or a shortlist. For the background, see Module 1 of D2.1 and D2.2, and Cases 1, 4 and 5 in D2.3.

Two limits matter most. First, even a well-built model predicts only moderately well: in a professional German academy, the best model reached an AUC (area under the curve) of 0.69, where 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect (Altmann et al., 2024). Second, most published talent-identification models have not been tested on outside data, and many have a high risk of bias (Tang et al., 2026). A tool trained on past selections can learn the biases in those selections.

Small-academy option

No AI tool? Use a structured scouting template: the same five to eight criteria, scored the same way, for every player. It builds the consistent data an AI tool would need later, and it addresses a problem named in section 2.1: two scouts rating the same player differently.

3.3 Interpreting AI outputs

Best practices for data interpretation
  1. A score is one input. Combine it with live observation and the scout's judgement (Altmann et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2026). The D2.3 practitioner cases (Cases 4, 5 and 7) reached the same conclusion: the data narrows the choice and people decide.
  2. Missing data is a hidden bias. Before trusting a ranking, check whose data is absent. D2.3 (Cases 4 and 7) warns that data supplied by interested parties, such as agents, can distort a shortlist.
  3. Check for the relative age effect. Players born early in the selection year are more likely to be selected, especially in adolescence; a 2023 meta-analysis of 90 football studies confirms the effect is strongest in adolescence and at higher levels (Cobley et al., 2009; Bozděch et al., 2023). An AI tool trained on past selections will learn this.
  4. Maturation is a separate bias. Relative age and biological maturity are two different effects, and a model can learn both. Check outputs by birth quarter and by maturity status (Hill et al., 2020). Approaches such as bio-banding exist, but evidence on their effects is still developing.
  5. Ask about calibration. A score of 0.8 should mean that about 8 in 10 such players were selected in the test data. If the supplier cannot explain how the probabilities were calibrated, treat the number as a rank, not a probability (Tang et al., 2026).
Watch out: automated decisions about children

Do not let the tool decide on its own whether a young player is selected or released. Section 2.5, principle 6, explains the legal basis (GDPR Art. 22 and Recital 71). A qualified person reviews the evidence, takes the decision and records it (Figure 3.1, callout 4).

3.4 Using AI tools in the scouting and training routine

Recommendations for integrating tools into training routines
  • Use the tool to build shortlists. Let it narrow 60 names to 15, then watch those 15 live.
  • Fix a review slot. One weekly shortlist review (about 20 minutes) where the named scout records a decision for every flagged player. Table 7.1 places it on MD-2 (two days before the match; see section 7.1), separate from the planning meeting.
  • Feed back to coaches in one line per player. "Flagged by the tool; watched twice; invite to trial" is enough. D2.3 (Cases 5, 6 and 8) found that players and coaches use short outputs and ignore long ones.
  • Keep the structured template running alongside. Your own consistent assessments are the data the tool needs, and your fallback if you stop using it.
  • Tell players and parents what the tool is. In simple words: it suggests who to watch, and the scout decides (section 2.5).

4.1 Overview: what wearable sensors measure

A GPS vest holds a small unit (the electronic module) on the player's upper back. The unit combines satellite positioning (GPS) with motion sensors such as an accelerometer (often grouped as an IMU, inertial measurement unit). Satellite positioning gives distance and speed outdoors. Motion-sensor metrics, such as accelerations and impacts, can still be used indoors, where GPS is not reliable (Malone et al., 2017; Torres-Ronda et al., 2022). A chest strap measures heart rate, which shows how hard the body is working.

These numbers describe external load (what the player did) and internal load (how the body responded). For the background, see Module 3 of the TalentTECH curriculum (D2.1) and the e-learning modules (D2.2). For examples from real clubs, see Cases 2, 7 and 11 in D2.3.

Small-academy option

No sensors yet? Start with session-RPE (RPE = rating of perceived exertion): after each session, each player rates how hard it was from 0 to 10. Multiply the rating by the session length in minutes. The method is validated and costs nothing (Haddad et al., 2017). See step 12 below.

4.3 Interpreting wearable data

Best practices for data interpretation

Wearable numbers are estimates with a margin of error (Torres-Ronda et al., 2022). Use them to plan and adjust training, with five rules in mind.

  1. Compare like with like. Do not compare numbers from different brands or systems, or from broadcast match tracking and your training vests, as if they were the same (Malone et al., 2017).
  2. Know what a "score" means. Before you use a brand's own load score, ask the supplier how it is calculated (Torres-Ronda et al., 2022).
  3. Read youth data in context. Interpret load in relation to each player's age, growth stage and fitness (Torres-Ronda et al., 2022).
  4. Check plan against reality. Compare the planned intensity (easy, medium, hard) with what players report. A large gap is a reason to talk, not to sanction (Haddad et al., 2017).
  5. Do not treat numbers as injury forecasts. Load monitoring helps you plan training. It cannot reliably tell you which player will be injured, so expect false alarms and missed cases (Bahr, 2016).
Watch out: the acute:chronic workload ratio

Some software shows an "acute:chronic workload ratio" (ACWR) as a red flag for injury. Do not use it that way. Researchers found conceptual and statistical problems with the ratio and have recommended that it be dropped (Impellizzeri et al., 2020; Impellizzeri et al., 2021).

Note on injury-prediction models

Some studies report promising injury-risk models built from GPS data, for example at a Polish professional club (Piłka et al., 2023; D2.3, Case 2). Results from one club's data need independent testing before they are trusted in a youth academy.

4.4 Using wearables in the training week

Recommendations for integrating tools into training routines
  • Same routine every time. Charge, fit, lock, record, download and check signal in the same order (section 4.2). A fixed routine reduces missing data.
  • Send a short report soon after the session. Give a few key numbers per player and one clear message (Torres-Ronda et al., 2022).
  • Name one data owner. This person downloads, checks and shares the data. Name a second person as backup.
  • Link it to the plan. Use the numbers when you plan the next sessions, not only when you review the last one. Chapter 7 shows a sample weekly cycle for all tools together.

5.1 Overview: what video analysis is used for in an academy

Academies use video for three things: reviewing team tactics, evaluating individual technique and decisions, and giving players feedback they can see. Systems range from a phone and free software to automated cameras that follow play and upload to the cloud. For the background, see Module 4 of D2.1 and D2.2, and Cases 3, 8, 10 and 12 in D2.3.

The hard parts are a stable camera position, written definitions for what you tag, and feedback that players accept. The setup steps below are built around them.

Small-academy option

A phone on a tall tripod at the halfway line, plus free video software, covers all twelve steps below. Add the automated camera later, once the routine works.

5.3 Interpreting video: consistent criteria and agreement between coders

Best practices for data interpretation
  1. Count only what you defined. A count of "good decisions" means nothing without a written definition of a good decision. Keep the tag list short and stable through the season.
  2. Check agreement regularly. Repeat the two-coder test (step 3) once a season, and whenever a new person starts tagging (Gong et al., 2019).
  3. Compare the player with their own earlier footage. Three clips from October next to three from March show development better than a comparison with a teammate (D2.3, Cases 6 and 7).
  4. Keep the full analysis for staff. The player sees the few clips; the coaching team sees the tagged timeline. Do not hand a 14-year-old a 90-minute review.
  5. Automated statistics are still counts. An automated camera's "passes" or "sprints" are produced by rules you did not write. Ask the supplier how each statistic is defined before you report it.
  6. Video can also develop the coach. A small study found that filming training helped youth coaches review their own coaching behaviour (Partington et al., 2015).

5.4 Using video in training and player feedback

Recommendations for integrating tools into training routines
  • Record every match, tag the ones you will use. Tagging takes far more time than recording, so decide each week which footage gets tagged and by whom.
  • One individual review per player per month is a realistic target for a small academy; this means a few players each week. Keep each review short and positive, with one learning point.
  • Link clips to the next session. "Here is the moment; here is the drill on Thursday" turns video into practice.
  • Psychological safety first. Players dislike critiquing teammates in front of the group (Middlemas & Harwood, 2018). Keep group sessions about the team shape, and individual points for individual meetings.
  • Adapt the format. For players with visual or hearing impairments, add spoken or written description to clips, and check with the player what works.

6 Bringing the data together: dashboards and the player profile

Chapters 3 to 5 each produce numbers. This chapter is about putting them next to each other without being misled. It applies the rule from section 2.5, principle 6, to all three sources: numbers describe the player; a person decides.

6.1 Combining AI, wearable and video data

Figure 6.1 shows the flow. Each source passes through a quality check by its data owner before it enters the player profile. From the profile come two things: a short report to the coach, and a decision taken by a person.

Three rules for combining:

  • Keep the sources separate in the profile. A GPS distance, an AI score and a tag count are different kinds of evidence. Show them side by side; do not add them into one "overall score".
  • Check each source on its own terms. Signal quality for wearables (section 4.2), data completeness for AI (section 3.2), tag agreement for video (section 5.2).
  • Do not mix systems. Numbers from different brands or systems are not comparable (section 4.3). The same applies to AI scores from different model versions and to tags from different definitions.

D2.3's Lithuanian cases (Cases 10–12) describe profiles that combine video, monitoring and emerging AI tools. They also note that some of this integration was still in development at the time of writing. Distinguish what you use now from what you plan to use.

6.2 Reading a performance dashboard

Most systems offer a dashboard. Use it in this order:

  1. Read the data quality indicators first. Missing sessions, poor signal, low completeness. If quality is poor, stop here for that player.
  2. Look at the trend. One high number after one session means little; four weeks in the same direction is worth acting on.
  3. Find out how each "score" is built. Suppliers' composite scores (load scores, readiness scores, talent scores) are formulas you did not write. Ask for the formula before you report the number (Torres-Ronda et al., 2022).
  4. Turn it into one message. A few key numbers per player and one clear sentence, soon after the session (Torres-Ronda et al., 2022). The dashboard serves the staff; the sentence serves the coach and the player.
  5. Treat every number as an estimate with error (Torres-Ronda et al., 2022).

6.3 Individual baselines and appropriate reference groups

The most useful comparison for a developing player is with their own earlier data. Build a baseline for each player over the first weeks of a season, and read later values against it. D2.3 (Cases 6 and 7) found that tracking change per player, rather than team averages, served development decisions better.

Group comparisons still have a place, but choose the group with care:

  • Same age group, same system, same season. A group that differs in any of these is not a fair reference.
  • Account for age and maturity. In youth players, read physical data in relation to age, growth stage and fitness (Torres-Ronda et al., 2022). For AI outputs, check by birth quarter and maturity status (Hill et al., 2020).
  • Use position groups for tactical video, not the whole squad.

A baseline cannot tell you who will be injured (Bahr, 2016) or who will be selected (Altmann et al., 2024). It shows whether this player is changing, and in which direction.

7.2 Match-day and post-match review workflow

  1. Before: camera at the marked position; units fitted and locked (if used); scouts know which flagged players to watch.
  2. During: record without stopping. Do not use live numbers to decide substitutions; the match is for coaching.
  3. Same day: upload and name the video; download and check wearable data.
  4. Next day: tag the match; export player clips; the data owner flags any quality problem.
  5. Within two days: one short report to the coaching team, and individual clips to a few players.

7.3 Feedback to players and parents

  • Players get short, individual and mostly positive feedback with one learning point, linked to the next session (Middlemas & Harwood, 2018). Give them numbers in context ("more than your October baseline"), not raw dashboards.
  • Parents are told what is collected, why, who sees it and how long it is kept (section 2.5). Give them a plain summary once or twice a season, without rankings.
  • Session-RPE lets you compare what the coach planned with what players report; treat a large gap as a reason to talk (section 4.3).
  • Never use data to punish. Players who experience video or numbers as punishment switch off (Middlemas & Harwood, 2018).

AccelerometerA motion sensor in a wearable unit that measures accelerations. It works indoors and outdoors.
ACWR (acute:chronic workload ratio)A ratio of recent to longer-term load shown in some software. Researchers have recommended that it be dropped as an injury flag (section 4.3).
AI literacyKnowing enough to use an AI tool in an informed way and to understand its risks (AI Act, Art. 4).
AUC (area under the curve)A measure of how well a model separates two groups. 0.5 is chance; 1.0 is perfect.
BaselineA player's own typical values, built over the first weeks of a season, used to read later values.
Bio-bandingGrouping players by biological maturity rather than chronological age. Evidence on its effects is still developing.
Biometric dataData from specific technical processing that uniquely identifies a person, for example face recognition. Ordinary video is not automatically biometric data.
CalibrationWhether a model's probabilities match reality: a score of 0.8 should mean about 8 in 10 such cases were positive.
Controller / processorUnder the GDPR, the controller decides why and how data is processed (usually the academy); a processor handles data on its behalf (often the supplier).
DeployerUnder the AI Act, an organisation that uses an AI system, for example an academy using a scouting tool.
DPA (data protection authority)The national supervisory authority for data protection.
DPIA (data protection impact assessment)A written assessment of risks to people before high-risk processing (GDPR, Art. 35).
DPO (data protection officer)The person responsible for data protection in an organisation. Not every academy must appoint one; every academy needs a named contact.
EPTSElectronic Performance and Tracking Systems: FIFA's term for wearable and optical tracking systems, with a quality programme and public test reports.
External loadWhat the player did: distance, speed, accelerations.
FirmwareThe software inside a device. Updates can change the numbers slightly.
GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the EU law on personal data. Partners in Türkiye follow KVKK instead.
GPSSatellite positioning. Gives distance and speed outdoors only.
HDOPA satellite-geometry quality value shown in some software. Lower is better.
Health dataPersonal data about physical or mental health. A special category under the GDPR (Art. 9). Sensor data collected over time is likely to count.
IMU (inertial measurement unit)The group of motion sensors in a wearable unit.
Internal loadHow the body responded: heart rate, session-RPE.
KVKKLaw No. 6698 on the Protection of Personal Data (Türkiye). The authority that enforces it carries the same abbreviation.
Legal basisOne of six grounds in the GDPR (Art. 6) on which personal data may be processed. Consent is one of them.
Maturation biasSelection advantage for early-maturing players. Separate from the relative age effect.
MD-n / MD+nMatch day minus or plus n days, for example MD-3 is three days before the match.
ProfilingAutomated processing of personal data to evaluate aspects of a person, such as performance or potential.
RecitalAn introductory paragraph of an EU regulation that explains the intent of its articles.
Relative age effectSelection advantage for players born early in the selection year. Strongest in adolescence.
Satellite lockWhen a GPS unit has found enough satellites to record position. Needs time outdoors before the session.
Session-RPE (rating of perceived exertion)A player's 0–10 rating of how hard the whole session was, multiplied by its length in minutes. A validated, no-cost measure of internal load.
TagA label placed on a moment in video, for example "shot". Every tag needs a written definition.
Two-coder agreementHaving two people tag the same footage and comparing the results to check that definitions are clear.
